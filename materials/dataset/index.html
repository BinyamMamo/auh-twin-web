The DCT Dataset: Structure, Coverage, and Practical Use

This document provides a complete guide to the competition dataset provided by the Abu Dhabi Department of Culture and Tourism (DCT). It details the files we received, explains every single column in plain language, reviews data coverage and quality quirks, and explains how we use each part in our models.

1. What Files We Received

The raw dataset was delivered as a compressed pack (01a - DCT Dataset-*.zip). When unpacked into data/dct/raw/, it contains the following files:

  1. data international_train.xlsx: Daily hotel guest records for 45 international nationalities from 1 January 2022 to 31 July 2025 (58,622 rows).
  2. data international_test.xlsx: Daily hotel guest records for 45 international nationalities for the test period, 1 August 2025 to 28 February 2026 (9,202 rows). In this file, the target variable (Guests) is withheld for scoring.
  3. data domestic_train.xlsx: Daily hotel guest records for domestic (in-country) UAE residents from 1 January 2022 to 31 July 2025 (1,308 rows).
  4. data domestic_test.xlsx: Daily hotel guest records for domestic visitors for the test period, 1 August 2025 to 28 February 2026 (212 rows), with Guests withheld.
  5. flight_data.xlsx: Historical and scheduled flight operations arriving into Abu Dhabi International Airport (AUH) from January 2022 to 28 February 2026 (117,608 rows).
  6. Data_Dictionary.pdf: The official reference document defining field definitions and sample values across the datasets.
  7. specs/: Folder containing competition challenge briefs and task specifications.

Cleaned Parquet Files

Because reading large Excel files is slow, our ingestion script in src/auhsim/dct.py parses and cleans the raw data into two fast, standardized Apache Parquet files: - data/dct/guests.parquet: Combined international and domestic hotel records (both train and test splits). - data/dct/flights.parquet: Cleaned flight operations data with standardized date formats and city-country mappings.

3. Time Coverage and Granularity

Understanding the timeline of each file is critical for structuring honest back-tests:

Hotel Data Timeline

  • Training Period: 1 January 2022 to 31 July 2025 (3 years and 7 months, or 1,308 calendar days).
  • 45 international nationalities: 58,622 rows (238 nationality-days are missing from a full grid).
  • Domestic: 1,308 rows (exactly one row per calendar day).
  • Both Guests and New Arrivals are fully populated.
  • Testing Period: 1 August 2025 to 28 February 2026 (7 months, or 212 calendar days).
  • 45 international nationalities: 9,202 rows.
  • Domestic: 212 rows.
  • New Arrivals, Same-Day Guests, and Date are provided. The target column Guests is withheld for competition scoring.

Flight Data Timeline

  • Historical Period: January 2022 to 28 February 2026 (117,608 rows across 130 departure cities in 33 countries, and dozens of airlines).
  • Granularity Shift:
  • 2022: Delivered as monthly totals stamped on the first day of each month at 12:00:00 AM.
  • 2023 to February 2026: Delivered at daily granularity for every individual city-airline flight record.
  • Future Scheduling: Flight rows extending from August 2025 to February 2026 represent published airline operational schedules.

4. What the Data Does Not Contain, and Why It Matters

Data science is often defined by what is missing. The DCT data pack has several important blind spots:

  1. No passenger nationality on flights: Flight manifests record where a plane departed, not the passports of the people on board. A German citizen boarding an Etihad flight in Bangkok appears only as a passenger arriving from Thailand.
  2. No true origin for connecting passengers: If a traveller flies from New York to Abu Dhabi via London on British Airways, the AUH flight record sees only a London departure. The ultimate origin of transfer passengers cannot be traced.
  3. No port of entry for hotel guests: Hotel check-in registers do not record how a visitor entered the UAE. A tourist who lands at Dubai International Airport (DXB) and takes a taxi to an Abu Dhabi resort appears in hotel statistics as a new arrival, but leaves no trace in AUH flight data.
  4. No land border traffic counts: GCC residents driving across the border from Saudi Arabia or Oman via highway checkpoints are not captured in flight statistics.
  5. Incomplete cabin breakdowns: Almost all cabin class capacity columns (Business Class Seat Capacity, Economy Class Seat Capacity, and all First Class columns) contain asterisks (*). We cannot reliably analyze premium versus budget seat shares.
  6. No room rates or hotel category tiers: The hotel files provide an aggregate headcount across all licensed accommodations in the emirate. There is no breakdown by luxury versus budget hotels, room rates, or geographical districts (such as Saadiyat Island, Yas Island, or Al Ain).

5. Data Quality Notes and Quirks

During exploratory data analysis (research/dct_eda.md), we uncovered several important patterns and data quirks:

1. Stock vs Flow: Guests vs New Arrivals

Guests is an in-house count (a stock: how many people are sleeping in hotel rooms tonight). New Arrivals is check-ins (a flow: how many people arrived today). Dividing total guests by daily arrivals yields an average ratio of roughly 3.7, which mirrors the average length of stay in Abu Dhabi hotels. On 99.2% of rows, New Arrivals is strictly less than or equal to Guests. The 0.8% of exceptions occur in small source markets with low volume or rapid same-day turnover.

2. Missing Nationality-Days in Train

A complete grid of 45 nationalities over 1,308 training days should contain 58,860 rows. The raw file contains 58,622 rows, meaning exactly 238 nationality-days are missing. Rather than leaving holes that break time-series lag calculations, our panel generator (src/auhsim/dct_models.py) re-indexes the data to a full daily grid. Missing arrival features are interpolated across gaps, while missing guest targets remain blank and are never scored.

3. Asterisk Placeholder (*)

An asterisk is used as a catch-all placeholder for zero, suppressed values (counts under 10 are withheld by the publisher for commercial privacy), or unrecorded data. Our cleaning pipeline converts asterisks to zero for count fields like Same-Day Guests, and converts them to missing values (NaN) for capacity fields.

4. International Doubled While Domestic Shrank

Between 2022 and 2025, tourism demand followed two opposite trends: - International daily guests doubled: averaging 13,000 guests per day in 2022, 18,200 in 2023, 23,500 in 2024, and 25,600 in early 2025. - Domestic daily guests declined: averaging 19,300 in 2022, 18,500 in 2023, 16,600 in 2024, and 15,600 in 2025. Because of this persistent trend, models trained only on the most recent 400 days consistently outperform models trained on all three and a half years of history.

5. Opposite Calendar Seasonalities

International and domestic tourists visit at opposite times of the year: - International tourism peaks in the cool winter months between October and February (peaking in December at index 1.16) and drops in the summer heat of June through September (dipping to 0.82). International travel shows virtually no day-of-week variation. - Domestic staycations peak in July and August (indexes 1.07 and 1.09) and December (1.08). Domestic travel is heavily weekend-driven, spiking on Fridays (1.08) and Saturdays (1.16) and dropping sharply on Sundays (0.89).

6. Wide Disparities in Length of Stay

Average length of stay differs by more than a factor of three across nationalities: - Short stays: Oman (1.6 nights), Qatar (2.1), China (2.3), Saudi Arabia (2.5), and UAE domestic residents (2.5). - Long stays: Germany, Russia, Egypt, Armenia, Finland, Sweden, and the UK (all averaging between 4.9 and 5.1 nights). Any flight change that alters the passenger mix shifts hotel room nights far more than a simple headcount suggests.

7. Rising Transfer Shares

About half of passengers arriving at Abu Dhabi International Airport do not enter the city. The point-to-point share dropped from 52.3% in 2023 to 49.6% in 2025, and reached 47.7% in early 2026. Connecting traffic is expanding faster than local traffic.

8. The Airspace Closure Shock

On 28 February 2026, the final day of the competition test window, a regional airspace closure occurred. In a single day, daily scheduled seats into AUH dropped from approximately 39,000 to 22,500, and daily point-to-point passengers fell from 13,200 to 7,900.

6. How We Use Each Part of the Data

To build an honest predictive system, we divide the data into two distinct operational pipelines:

Competition Dataset
   ├── Daily Hotel Arrivals + Lags + Calendars ──→ Nowcast Model (8.29% WMAPE)
   └── Monthly Flight Schedules + P2P + Capture ──→ Simulator Chain (19.16% WMAPE)

The Nowcast Pipeline

  • Objective: Predict daily in-house guests for 45 international nationalities plus domestic over the 212 test days.
  • Inputs Used: Provided New Arrivals, Same-Day Guests, lag features (arrivals over the past 14 days, rolling sums over 7, 14, and 28 days), tomorrow's arrivals, daily point-to-point passengers from that country's direct flights, and calendar variables (day of week, month, day of year, UAE public holidays, Ramadan, and Eid).
  • Key Feature: Because hotel check-ins are known in the test files, the nowcast relies heavily on the stay-curve relationship between check-ins and in-house guests.

The Simulator Pipeline

  • Objective: Predict monthly hotel guest nights 12 months ahead under hypothetical flight schedule modifications, where future hotel check-ins are completely unknown.
  • Inputs Used: Historical monthly flight schedules (seats), baseline load factors, baseline point-to-point shares, fitted capture parameters (a_own, a_other, and ground baseline b), and seasonal length of stay.
  • Key Feature: Uses the R2-A anchored chain to scale last year's actual hotel nights by the flight schedule's predicted change.

Preventing Data Leakage

To ensure that our reported back-test accuracy is genuine: - In all evaluation windows (W0, W1, W2, W3), every model is trained strictly on data dated prior to the forecast cutoff date. - Future load factors and point-to-point shares are not assumed to be known. In the schedule-only information set (S), they are taken from the exact same calendar month one year earlier. - Window W0 (July 2023 to January 2024) was kept completely clean as an un-inspected verification check.

7. Ideas for How Else the Data Could Be Used

Beyond the core competition goals, the rich DCT data pack opens several high-value avenues for future exploration:

  1. Airline Route Prioritization and Incentive Design: By combining flight load factors with hotel capture and length of stay, aviation authorities can identify routes that deliver the highest local economic value per seat. For example, attracting flights from long-stay markets (like Germany or the UK) generates significantly more economic activity than short-stay transit corridors.
  2. Stopover Conversion Campaigns: With over 50% of arriving passengers transferring to onward destinations, converting just 2 to 3 percentage points of transfer traffic into 48-hour stopovers would inject tens of thousands of new hotel guest nights into the emirate without needing a single new aircraft or runway slot.
  3. Hotel Revenue Management and Seasonal Pricing: Because international tourists and domestic residents have completely inverted seasonal calendars, hotel operators could use our models to dynamically adjust room rates and marketing channels, pivoting toward domestic staycations in summer weekends and international packaging in winter.
  4. Crisis Resilience and Stranded Passenger Management: The flight transfer data can be combined with airport distance clusters to model distressed passenger hotel room demand during weather disruptions or regional airspace closures, ensuring airport hotels are prepared before terminals become congested.
